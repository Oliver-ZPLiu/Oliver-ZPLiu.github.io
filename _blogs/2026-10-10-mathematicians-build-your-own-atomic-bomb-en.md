As someone who was trained in quantum theory and has temporarily stepped away from the field after weighing, among other considerations, AI's potential impact, I am not particularly surprised by OpenAI's recent moves. Perhaps the only surprise is that this moment has arrived sooner than I expected.

<!--more-->

For now, the most realistic response available to the mathematics community, and perhaps the one most likely to make a practical difference, is to actively embrace open-source models and train frontier models of its own, guided by the community's understanding of academic standards and the nature of mathematics itself. After all, if we cannot stop AI from advancing, we might as well take an active part in shaping its direction.

This brings to mind the Manhattan Project. Faced with the threat that an opposing camp might acquire nuclear weapons first, physicists actively pushed for their own side to develop them independently. The two situations may not be so different. The problem is that acting now may already be somewhat late. More importantly, who has the resolve and sense of responsibility to rally the community and play the role Einstein played back then?

To put it somewhat provocatively, I would even suggest that everyone working in mathematics around the world contribute half a year's income and make the specialized data they have accumulated fully available and licensed for use. Together, they could raise funding on the order of $10 billion and build a model research and development program led by the mathematics community, using open-source models as its foundation, to take on OpenAI directly in terms of model capabilities. This would probably have far more practical value than calls for a boycott.

In fact, every field of scientific research that could face disruptive upheaval from AI should consider taking similar action.

I also believe that practitioners across these fields need to make further changes in their mindset and how they define their roles. Simply recognizing AI's power and actively using AI tools in everyday research may already be far from enough.

Many domain experts need to redefine their roles: from researchers working independently of AI, or "AI for XXX" specialists primarily designing harnesses and building workflows for general-purpose models, toward data experts who participate directly in building the capabilities of models specialized for their domains. The clearest distinction here is whether your own field or AI sits at the center of your work.

The reason is clear: since the second half of this year began, advances in AI's recursive self-improvement (RSI) capabilities have accelerated model iteration. Intelligence density is rising rapidly, while inference costs continue to fall. The only significant bottlenecks constraining the pace are compute and the construction of the infrastructure needed to provide it.

At this pace of iteration, it may no longer be enough for domain experts to channel their professional value solely into harness engineering that constrains and stabilizes model capabilities. Models are evolving rapidly and gradually internalizing some of the capabilities that previously depended on external harnesses. In the near term, a more worthwhile investment is to use deep domain insight to continually identify and develop valuable hard cases and research scenarios beyond the capability boundaries of frontier models, then turn them into training data, evaluation frameworks, and feedback mechanisms.

It is foreseeable that most disciplines could undergo similar changes. If this trend continues, then, to put it more radically, perhaps there will no longer be independent "fields of XX" in the sense we understand them today. Instead, they may increasingly take the form of "AI applied to XX."

Today, it is mathematics and theoretical research. What comes next? Computational simulation? Experimental design? How far away is the day when these fields reach the same turning point?

The same principle applies: the best response to nuclear deterrence is to possess the technology yourself.
